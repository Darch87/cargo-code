Re-Wilding Detroit
This design studio explored what happens when urbanism is decoupled from the concept of economic growth. It investigated alternatives to the current world paradigm that favors unbridled development over social well-being and environmental sustainability. The studio embraced obsolescence and “unbuilding” as urban strategies to develop spatial networks that seek balance and equilibrium over mindless expansion.

Subtractive design tactics, downscaling strategies, disassembly logics and soft construction methods were used to design with Detroit’s existing abandoned building stock, minimizing the use of resources while experimenting with processes of adaptation. This studio explored the formal and figural language of off-the-shelf construction elements, from everyday bricks, aluminum studs and gypsum board to hidden plumbing pipes, electrical boxes and HVAC ducts. Rather than following prescribed norms of assembly, we treated these elements as expressive characters, each with its own geometries, formal properties and material expression, and reconfigured them into new assemblies that push the boundaries of tectonics.

Offramp 15: Stuff
SCI-Arc
As we fling around the term “object” within architectural discourse, questioning its relationship to other things, perhaps it’s time to critically step back. Take a step back and look at all the stuff we have accumulated.

Stuff—the name we use to describe the indescribable, unspecific, imprecise—encompasses our overwhelming inability to consider things individually. However, stuff is not things. Stuff flattens things, it generalizes objects into a flat ontology and isn’t ashamed to admit it. Stuff is also versatile, and dimensional, in that it can collect miscellaneous entities across vast stretches of the universe in the simple use of any random adjective: Fun Stuff. Pink Stuff. That Stuff.

Stuff is unmeasurable, invisible. It can be matter, energy, space, collections, aggregates. Stuff can be old, new, borrowed, or blue. Stuff is inclusive and diverse. Stuff is microscopic and stuff is bigger than the universe. Stuff has smoky boundaries, stuff is shapeless. Stuff is everything and nothing.

Energyscapes
Free School of Architecture
A workshop with the Free School of Architecture and the Woodbury School of Architecture Gallery (WUHO) taught students about contemporary environmental sensing devices, using accessible tools to map the building’s energyscapes.

A point cloud was generated from 360-degree video using photogrammetry, giving a user-made digital model from simple, affordable scanning technology. Students then measured the space with a handheld weather meter, recording temperature and humidity throughout the building, and layered this data onto the point cloud to make a digital landscape where energy can be seen and understood. The workshop asked students to relate abstract concepts like temperature to visual and spatial systems, so that they design through not just form but energy.

Offramp 14: Crowds
SCI-Arc
‘The crowd’ can be understood as both object and experience: viewed from a distance, from above, as data, pressing in from all sides, pushing towards the exit, or up against the barricade. How to figure the crowd, and to what end, depends on the contingency of where one stands. Variously identified as the population, the masses, or the multitude, the crowd remains an urgent category of contemporary aesthetics and politics, yet is often undervalued in architectural discourse today.

If social thought at the turn of the twentieth century understood the crowd as a figure to be feared and suppressed, we now speak of both ‘crowd intelligence’ and ‘smart cities’ as a new data-driven optimism towards the crowd and its algorithmic management. Politically transformative and spatially ambiguous but increasingly measurable, the crowd is the client, the brief, and the site: the universal basic datum of mass culture and contemporary urbanism.

The project for Hello Wood’s Project Village II turns the idea of the Migrant House into a dynamic concept of ‘Migrant Hous(ing)’. Each person arrives at the site with their own unit, a series of rotating frames that can be configured into many spaces based on personal need. The units have material limits that keep anyone from building a complete house alone. But as people form relationships (friendships, groups, romantic entanglements), the units can transform. Only through a collective force can they reach their full structural potential and endless combinations, testing the true nature of community building.

With: Chair of Architecture and Urban Design, ETH Zurich
Client: Hello Wood
Team Leaders: Danny Wills, Gian Maria Socci

Empower Shack
Cape Town
Empower Shack is an interdisciplinary development project directed by ETH and the local NGO Ikhayalami Development Services, with the BT-Section community and local and international partners. Its pilot phase focused on a cluster of 68 houses in the BT-Section of Khayelitsha, developing an informal settlement upgrading strategy built on four parts: a two-story housing prototype, participatory spatial planning, ecological landscape management and integrated livelihoods programming. Using the “Blocking Out” process with new digital visualization tools and micro-financing, it created an interface between residents, professionals and the government.

A main focus is sustainable landscape elements that reduce the use of water and other services through low-tech solutions such as capturing, treating and reusing greywater. Water-sensitive features like tree pits and reed channels also control flooding. These were designed directly with residents so they would work in practice.

The Public Spectacle
UC Berkeley / Swissnex
City sidewalks have become contested zones, where pedestrians usually lose out to cars and other traffic. The “parklet” model started by Rebar is a simple, “user-generated” way to expand the public realm where sidewalks are too narrow to support a vibrant street life. The Public Spectacle parklet, commissioned in 2014 by Swissnex San Francisco and developed with Jan Gehl Architects, builds on this idea by offering an adaptable space for public exchange and new forms of civic agency.

It was designed with students from ETH and UC Berkeley through participatory building workshops. After a weeklong seminar on placemaking and public space, students designed and built a series of furniture pieces to complement the main parklet.

Cultivating the Map
Student work, The Cooper Union
The way we live leaves traces on the Earth. The tools we use to situate ourselves in the landscape leave marks and inscriptions of these processes on the ground, becoming a direct reflection of the society that formed it. The map acts as a record of these inscriptions, the outcome of a gathering of elements for the construction of a narrative about the site’s reality. This thesis finds itself in the territory of the map, proposing that the map is also a generative tool. Using the drawing as fertile ground, this thesis attempts a predictive organization of territory through the design of four new tools for the management of natural resources in the Great Plains, a region threatened with the cumulative adverse effects of industrial farming. Each tool proposes new ways of drawing the land and acts as an instrument that reveals the landscape’s new potential.

Architecture at Cooper
The Cooper Union
Architecture at Cooper 1859 - 2009 traces the successive transformations of Cooper Union’s architecture, beginning with its landmarked, 19th century brownstone Foundation Building designed by Frederick Petersen -- extensively renovated by Leopold Eidlitz in the 1880s and 90s; transformed internally in 1975 by John Hejduk; and restored externally in 2002 -- to its new academic building at 41 Cooper Square designed by Thom Mayne, and completed in 2009. The exhibition also examines how the education of architects -- beginning with the first drawing classes of 1860 to the five year undergraduate professional degree program and post-professional master’s program -- has been informed and structured within the careful design and re-design of its buildings.

With: The Irwin S. Chanin School of Architecture at The Cooper Union
Curator: Steven Hillyer
Research and Digital Animation: Danny Wills and Rolando Vega

Chelsea Kunsthalle
Student work, The Cooper Union
The program was a small exhibition hall along the lines of a European “Kunsthalle,” which mounts temporary art exhibitions ranging from works on paper to large sculptural and architectural objects, along with a collections library, classrooms, an auditorium, a project room for visiting artists, a café and staff offices. The private, secondary spaces were formally separated from the galleries into seven curvilinear “pods” that cut through the regular floor slabs and perimeter walls, with circulation tucked into the poché of the pods for an uninterrupted experience of each space.

Kern Oil ®
In the dystopian spirit of Monsanto, Nestle and Chevron, this project imagines Kern County, California, as a region where water is entirely privately owned, whether by a corporation, a small farmer or a suburban homeowner flushing their own toilet. All water, from fresh to grey and brackish, becomes a valued commodity whose worth rises with its scarcity. Yet because clear lines of ownership can’t be drawn around such a viscous material, Kern County is forced to become an integrated machine of water sharing.

The project layers existing technologies and recycling techniques and pushes them to their extreme. Brackish water from oil drilling irrigates farms, shifting seismic activity creates new basins, suburbia recycles bodily water for home gardens, and the city’s smog shades and fertilizes urban farms. Meanwhile, genetic modification has produced the super-kernel, or “Kern-Oil,” a crop that absorbs, recycles and distributes water and serves as biofuel, medicine, and animal and human feed.

Santa Maria in Campitelli
The Cooper Union
Santa Maria in Campitelli, Carlo Rainaldi’s 17th-century Baroque church, was analyzed through its key conceptual strategies. A typology of its many pilaster and column conditions looked for a logic in their geometric configurations, and a series of operations on a model of the church transformed its spaces to understand how they relate. This revealed that the church joins two distinct church types: the basilica nave with side aisles and the central-cross plan. Abstract drawings and models then explored how these two strategies play against and with each other throughout the church.

Terra-Techtonics
The Cooper Union
A tool made for measuring the site recorded a series of dimensions radiating from one point. Turned into orthographic drawings, these notations revealed a section of maximum and minimum elevation and a section of nearly flat elevation, 60 degrees apart. These became the two main axes: the cardinal axis and the topological axis.

This led to a program of two opposing uses forced to coexist. The photographer enters his private studio house along the cardinal axis, then shifts to live and work in the topological axis. The artist works on display to the gallery, reached from the topological axis, then shifts to the cardinal axis. The two never physically meet but constantly envelop one another in space.
